Part 4 · Running the project4.1 — Who owns what
4.1

Who owns what

We own parts of the system, not tasks. That way nobody has to ask whose decision something is, and we rarely edit the same file at the same time.

Stream A

The engine

Owns the loop, the item model and expressions. Writes Phase 1 alone, then supports everyone else. This is the technical-lead seat, and the one place two people editing at once produces a mess.

Stream B

The canvas

Owns everything in the browser: the drawing surface, the settings-panel generator, the run viewer, the code box. The largest single piece of work in the project.

Stream C

The server

Owns the five addresses, webhook handling, the schedule timers and the Google permission flow. The trickiest debugging in the project lands here.

Stream D

The nodes

Owns every integration. Cannot start properly until Phase 3 is finished — before that, writes node descriptions and does the Google Cloud setup.

Stream E

Infrastructure, testing and the report

Owns Docker, deployment, the test setup and the benchmark, and is editor-in-chief of the report and director of the demo. Starts on day one, not in Week 12 — the report is written a section a week from Week 5 onwards.

The sequencing mistake to avoidDo not put five people on nodes in Week 5. Until the skeleton exists there is nothing to build against, and parallel work on an unstable core produces five rewrites. Weeks 4–5 are A and C building the spine while B builds the canvas, D writes node descriptions and does the Google setup, and E stands up Docker and the automatic checks. Real parallel work starts once Phase 3 lands.
Part 4 · Running the project4.2 — Risks and habits
4.2

Three risks that end projects, and six habits that save them

Google keys expire after seven days

While our app is in Testing mode the long-lived key stops working after a week. Connected in Week 9, dead by Week 14 — and it looks like a broken node, not an expired key.

What we do: reconnect every Google account on the morning of the demo. It is step one of the checklist, not a footnote.

Gmail permissions are restricted

Making the app public needs a Google security review we cannot complete this semester.

What we do: stay in Testing mode and add every member — plus our demonstrator — as test users in Week 7, not in Week 8 when we first need it.

Nothing works on demo day

Campus wifi, an API outage, a laptop that decides to update. All of it happens, and always at nine in the morning.

What we do: record a full video of the working demo in Week 13, bring a second laptop, and have a phone hotspot ready.

Six habits that will save us weeks

  • 01Build the boring version first. An HTTP node that only does the simplest request is worth more in Week 6 than a perfect one in Week 9, because everything downstream can start.
  • 02One person owns the engine. Two people editing it at once produces a mess nobody can untangle.
  • 03We are not building user accounts. Logins and password resets earn no marks here and will eat a week.
  • 04When a node will not work, test the plain request in Postman first. Most “my node is broken” turns out to be a missing permission or a wrong header — nothing to do with our code.
  • 05Print the data between nodes, constantly. Almost every confusing bug is the shape of the data, not the logic — and section 1.3 explains the shape it should have.
  • 06Copy n8n's good ideas deliberately, and say so in the report. Explaining why a design is right is exactly what the assignment asks for. Copying without understanding is what costs marks.
If we fall behindWe cut nodes, never phases. Losing the Drive node costs almost nothing — it is one row in the acceptance checklist. Losing Phase 3 means every later node needs a hand-built settings panel and only one person can work at a time, which would cost us the whole of Weeks 8 to 10. The order of what to drop, if it comes to it: Drive, then Docs, then the second half of the benchmark, then the Code node. Never the AI node, and never a phase.

Our weekly rhythm from hereMonday, thirty minutes: agree this week's “done when”. Thursday, thirty minutes: everyone shows what actually runs, and one person clones the repository fresh and starts it. Nothing else. Two meetings a week is enough if both end in something running.
